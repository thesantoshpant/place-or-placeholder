Paper 2 — Fine-tuned Satellite Model for Dengue Forecasting: Build
Plan
Nov 15, 2026 · @Adithya
Summary
Paper 2 builds a model that learns dengue-relevant features straight from satellite images and uses them
to forecast weekly district cases. It is submitted around September 2027 and runs on the team's RTX
A6000 in about 300–600 GPU-hours.
Working title: Learning where dengue grows: fine-tuning an Earth-observation foundation model for
district-level forecasting.
What we build:
1. A satellite encoder: Prithvi-EO-2.0 (300M parameters, open licence) fine-tuned with low-rank
adapters (LoRA) on NASA's Harmonized Landsat-Sentinel (HLS) image chips.
2. A learned pooling layer that decides which chips in a district matter, instead of a plain average.
3. A forecaster that combines the district's image features, past cases, climate and its neighbours'
cases, and outputs weekly case ranges 1–4 weeks ahead.
Why it is new: recent dengue papers use frozen, pre-computed embeddings averaged over a district.
None fine-tunes a satellite foundation model on dengue at national scale, learns which places inside a
district matter, or tests transfer to another continent.
The test it must pass: beat Paper 1's best model (LightGBM with AlphaEarth embeddings), using Paper
1's splits and location-only controls, including on Taiwan, which it never sees in training.
Target venues: ML4H 2027 (full paper; deadline expected early September) or the second KDD 2028
cycle. A health-journal companion on hospital access follows (see Companion study).
Novelty vs prior work
No paper we found trains a satellite encoder on dengue at national scale, learns which places inside a
district matter, or tests on another continent. Paper 2 does all three.

Architecture
The model has two parts. An image path turns each district's satellite chips into one learned feature
vector per year. A forecaster joins that vector with the district's case history and its neighbours to predict
weekly cases.

Paper 2 model · image path and forecaster
The highlighted encoder is the only expensive part, and it runs once per setting. The forecaster on the
right is small and trains in an hour or two. That split is what keeps the project inside one A6000.
Data and imagery pipeline
The new work in Paper 2 is about 1.6 million satellite image chips, roughly 1.0 TB at full size. Case,
climate and population tables are reused from Paper 1 unchanged. The pipeline starts in January 2027,
while Paper 1 experiments run.
Imagery source: Harmonized Landsat Sentinel-2 (HLS) v2 from Microsoft Planetary Computer. It is free,
30 m resolution, with images every 2–3 days, and has the six bands Prithvi was pretrained on (blue,
green, red, near-infrared, two shortwave infrared).
How chips are made:
1. Pick places. In each district, sample up to 16 chip centres, weighted by population (WorldPop)
and built-up area (GHSL). Districts with few people get fewer chips. Paper 1's RQ5 results decide
whether to over-sample fast-growing urban edges.
2. Pick dates. Make two cloud-masked median composites per year, one for the wet season and
one for the dry season, using HLS's own cloud mask (Fmask).
3. Cut chips. 224 × 224 pixels (about 6.7 km on a side), 6 bands, stored as 16-bit integers in Zarr
shards by country and year.
4. Years. 2017–2024, so each forecast year can use the previous year's images, as in Paper 1.

never re-downloads.
Training and experiments
Training happens in three stages, so the expensive image step runs once per setting instead of inside
every forecast run. Every result is scored on Paper 1's splits, metrics and controls.
Stage A: teach the encoder about dengue (about 50–70 GPU-hours)
•
•
•
•
Input: all chips for one district-year.
Task: predict that district's dengue incidence for the year (log cases per 100,000).
Model: Prithvi-EO-2.0 with LoRA adapters (rank 8–16 on attention layers, about 1% of weights
trained), then attention pooling across chips.
Train on Brazil, Colombia and Peru for 2018–2019 only, so no test year leaks into the encoder. For
the spatial splits (S2–S5), Stage A is re-run per fold on training districts only; otherwise held-out
districts leak in through their 2018–2019 labels.
Stage B: extract features (about 4 GPU-hours per pass)
•
Run the tuned encoder once over every district-year. Save one feature vector per district-year,
plus the pooling weights for each chip.
Stage C: train the forecaster (about 1–2 GPU-hours per run)
•
•
•
•
•
A temporal transformer reads the past 52 weeks of cases and climate for each district.
Graph attention adds information from neighbouring districts (shared borders plus 5 nearest).
The year's image features enter as one extra token.
Output: 23 quantiles for 1–4 weeks ahead, trained with quantile (pinball) loss, so the WIS from
Paper 1 applies directly.
Optional final step: a short end-to-end fine-tune of Stages A and C together, if the May gate
shows headroom.

Taiwan transfer
•
•
•
Zero-shot: apply the model trained on the three Latin American countries to Taiwan, with no
Taiwan data in training.
Few-shot: fine-tune only the forecaster on 1 and 2 years of Taiwan data.
Report both against Taiwan-only LightGBM. This is the paper's strongest claim if it works, and an
honest limit if it does not.
Interpretation: map the pooling weights. If the model attends to urban edges, informal housing and
water bodies, that matches known dengue ecology and supports the companion study.
Companion study: dengue and hospital access
A second, health-focused paper asks whether forecast dengue surges fall hardest on districts that are far
from hospitals, and how that differs between rural and urban areas. It reuses Paper 2's forecasts, so it
costs almost no extra compute. The healthcare lead owns it, from August to October 2027.
Questions
1. Do rural districts with long travel times to care have more severe dengue (more hospitalizations
or deaths per reported case)?
2. During forecast surge weeks, how many people live more than 60 minutes from a hospital in the
districts with the highest forecast cases?
3. Does the urban–rural gap in severe outcomes shrink or grow over 2013–2021 (the years with
hospitalization data)?
