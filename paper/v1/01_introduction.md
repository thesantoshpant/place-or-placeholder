## 1 Introduction

Dengue causes a large burden of illness in the tropics and subtropics. The Global Burden of Disease study estimated [to be completed] dengue cases and [to be completed] deaths worldwide in [to be completed] [@gbd2023diseases; @ihme2023gbdresults]. <!-- AUTHORS: GBD numbers (cases, deaths and reference year) to be pulled from the IHME GBD Results tool; no figure has been verified --> Recent seasons were unusually large. Peru had the largest dengue outbreak on its record in 2023 [@munayco2024peru], and Brazil its largest epidemic to date in 2024 [@gurgelgoncalves2024brazil]. Short-term forecasts for small areas can help health services time vector control and prepare clinical care. Dengue forecasts that give a range of likely values, rather than a single number, are well established [@johansson2019challenge; @lowe2016elife; @colongonzalez2021vietnam; @araujo2026sprint].

Dengue transmission depends partly on climate and the environment [@lowe2016elife; @colongonzalez2021vietnam] and on human movement [@harish2024movement]. Forecasters therefore often add descriptors of place from separate data products. Geospatial foundation models offer a shortcut. Trained on large archives of satellite and other observations, they compress them into a fixed-length list of numbers, called an embedding, for any location. AlphaEarth Foundations provides a 64-number embedding for every 10 m pixel and every year from 2017 [@brown2025alphaearth; @aef_dataset]. It is computed from optical and radar satellite imagery by a model that was also trained to reconstruct climate, elevation and other layers. <!-- src: docs/research/literature.md verification round 2 item 4 (V1 ANNUAL, unit-length, 2017 onward); docs/research/data_and_tools.md AlphaEarth row (bands A00–A63, 10 m); inputs vs training targets: Brown et al. arXiv:2507.22291v2 Table S1 and section S15, read 2026-11-18 --> The Population Dynamics Foundation Model (PDFM) provides place embeddings built from search, map and activity data [@agarwal2024pdfm]. Even generic image features predict many outcomes [@rolf2021mosaiks; @lacoste2023geobench]. Because embeddings cover the whole globe, they are attractive where local data are sparse.

An embedding can also act as a fingerprint of a district. A district's embedding changes little from year to year, so a flexible model can use it to recognise the district and learn its usual dengue level without using what it says about the environment. The embedding is then only a placeholder: a label that tells the model which district it is looking at. When every test district also appears in training, this labelling role alone can improve scores. We call this the identity confound. In spatial machine learning, coordinates and similar stand-ins for place help on places a model has seen but work poorly on new ones [@meyer2019spatial; @mila2024proxies], and spatial validation is debated [@roberts2017cv; @ploton2020spatial; @wadoux2021spatialcv]. In England, much of the gain from satellite-image embeddings for house prices, but not for air pollution, reflected coarse location [@metzler2026satellite].

The identity confound can be tested with location-only features and with places never seen in training. Dengue adds a complication. Once a model sees each district's recent case counts, the case history already identifies the district. Holding districts out of training stops a model from memorising which embedding belongs to which district, but a held-out district still brings its own case history. The only clean test of whether embeddings carry environmental information or merely identify a district is therefore one without case history.

Published dengue evaluations of place embeddings have not yet applied these tests. Brittain et al. added one PDFM snapshot to monthly forecasts for 5,570 Brazilian municipalities as a residual adjustment, with temporal hold-outs only [@brittain2026foundation]. Forecast error fell in 19 of 20 combinations of model and horizon, and we found no location-only control in the study. <!-- src: docs/research/literature.md verification round 2 item 1 --> In the Mexico dengue case study of Muslim et al., a static embedding reduced the mean weighted interval score by 0.0051 at one month, and the gain was not significant at three or six months [@muslim2026planetary]. That case study had no location-only control or held-out region, although other case studies in the same paper had such checks. <!-- src: docs/research/literature.md verification round 2 item 2 --> For two other outcomes, malaria and respiratory infection, Nazir et al. reported AlphaEarth gains [@nazir2026alphaearth; @nazir2026malaria]. We found no control based on coordinates, district labels or shuffled embeddings in these studies, and no spatial hold-out. The closest precedent is a single-city study in India, where a satellite-embedding advantage over open map layers "fell to 0.001 once the open layers were given the cell's own coordinates" [@pradhan2026benchmarks]. We extend that test to four countries, to weekly forecasts that give a range of values, and to places held out of training.

In a retrospective study, we tested whether AlphaEarth embeddings improve weekly dengue forecasts in 6,909 scored second-level administrative units of Brazil, Colombia, Peru and Taiwan. <!-- src: S7_embedding_distance.csv n_units BR_S5 5,439 + CO_S5 1,070 + PE_S5 115 + TW_S5 285 = 6,909; Table 1 note explains the 6,990 reporting and 6,987 mapped districts --> These are municipalities in Brazil and Colombia, provinces in Peru and townships in Taiwan; we call them all districts. A location control is an input that tells the model where a district is, which district it is, or how much dengue it usually has, without describing its own environment directly. We report what the embeddings add to the full model, with and without such a control, in future years of the same districts and in states, regions, districts or a whole country held out of training.

We asked four questions. When the test covers only future years, do embeddings reduce forecast error beyond the other inputs? Is any gain left once a location control is present? Does any gain appear in districts, states or countries never seen in training? Finally, in exploratory analyses, do embeddings predict a district's average dengue burden, and without case history, do they add anything beyond location?
