## 1 Introduction

Dengue causes a large burden of illness in the tropics and subtropics. Short-term forecasts for small areas can help health services time vector control and prepare clinical care, and probabilistic dengue forecasting is well established [@johansson2019challenge; @lowe2016elife; @colongonzalez2021vietnam; @araujo2026sprint].

Dengue transmission depends partly on the environment and on human movement [@harish2024movement]. Forecasters therefore often add descriptors of place, each from its own data product. Geospatial foundation models offer a shortcut: they compress large archives of observations into a fixed-length list of numbers, called an embedding, for any location. AlphaEarth Foundations provides a 64-number embedding for every 10 m pixel and every year from 2017 [@brown2025alphaearth; @aef_dataset]. It is computed from optical and radar satellite imagery by a model that was also trained to reconstruct climate, elevation and other layers. <!-- src: docs/research/literature.md verification round 2 item 4 (V1 ANNUAL, unit-length, 2017 onward); docs/research/data_and_tools.md AlphaEarth row (bands A00–A63, 10 m); inputs vs training targets: Brown et al. arXiv:2507.22291v2 Table S1 and section S15, read 2026-11-18 --> The Population Dynamics Foundation Model (PDFM) provides place embeddings built from search, map and activity data [@agarwal2024pdfm]. Generic image features, even random ones, predict many outcomes [@rolf2021mosaiks], and benchmarks compare such models on Earth-observation tasks [@lacoste2023geobench]. Embeddings exist everywhere, so they are attractive where local data are thin.

An embedding is also a fingerprint. A district's embedding changes little from year to year, so a flexible model can use it to recognise the district and learn its usual dengue level, ignoring what it says about the environment. When every test district also appears in training, this identifier role alone can improve scores. We call this the identity confound. In spatial machine learning, coordinates and similar proxies raise apparent skill but extrapolate poorly [@meyer2019spatial; @mila2024proxies], and spatial validation is debated [@roberts2017cv; @ploton2020spatial; @wadoux2021spatialcv]. Learned location encoders can stand in for place [@russwurm2024location; @vivanco2023geoclip]. In England, much of the gain from satellite-image embeddings for house prices, though not for air pollution, reflected coarse location [@metzler2026satellite].

The identity confound can be tested with location-only features and with places never seen in training. Dengue adds a complication: once a model sees each district's recent case counts, the case history already identifies the district. Holding districts out of training stops a model from memorising which embedding belongs to which district, but a held-out district still brings its own case history. Whether embeddings act as environmental information or as an identifier can therefore be tested cleanly only without case history.

Published dengue evaluations of place embeddings have not yet applied these tests. Brittain et al. added one PDFM snapshot to monthly forecasts for 5,570 Brazilian municipalities as a residual adjustment, with temporal hold-outs only [@brittain2026foundation]; scores fell in 19 of 20 model-horizon combinations, and we found no location-only control. <!-- src: docs/research/literature.md verification round 2 item 1 --> In the Mexico dengue case study of Muslim et al., a static embedding reduced the mean weighted interval score by 0.0051 at one month, and the gain was not significant at three or six months [@muslim2026planetary]. That case study had no location-only control or held-out region, unlike others in the paper. <!-- src: docs/research/literature.md verification round 2 item 2 --> Nazir et al. reported AlphaEarth gains for malaria and respiratory infection without a coordinate, identifier or shuffled-embedding control and without a spatial hold-out [@nazir2026alphaearth; @nazir2026malaria]. The closest precedent is a single-city study in India, where a satellite-embedding advantage over open map layers "fell to 0.001 once the open layers were given the cell's own coordinates" [@pradhan2026benchmarks]. We extend that test to four countries, weekly probabilistic forecasts and held-out places.

We evaluated, retrospectively, whether AlphaEarth embeddings add skill to weekly dengue forecasts in 6,909 scored second-level administrative units of Brazil, Colombia, Peru and Taiwan. <!-- src: S7_embedding_distance.csv n_units BR_S5 5,439 + CO_S5 1,070 + PE_S5 115 + TW_S5 285 = 6,909; Table 1 note explains the 6,990 reporting and 6,987 mapped districts --> These are municipalities in Brazil and Colombia, provinces in Peru and townships in Taiwan; we call them all districts. Models added one block of inputs at a time, ending with the previous year's embeddings, with configuration and scored rows fixed within each country. We report what the embeddings add to the full model, without and with a location control (a district's position, pre-study dengue level, identifier or a shuffled embedding), when new years, states, regions, districts or a whole country are held out. Exploratory analyses dropped case history, to see whether identifier and place information can be told apart, and asked whether the embeddings predict a district's average dengue burden at all.

We asked four questions. When only the years are new, do embeddings reduce forecast error beyond the other inputs? Is any gain left once a location control is present? Does any gain appear in districts, states or countries never seen in training? And, exploratorily, without case history, do embeddings add anything beyond location?
