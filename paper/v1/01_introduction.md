## 1 Introduction

Dengue causes a large burden of illness in the tropics and subtropics. An estimated 390 million dengue infections occur each year, and 96 million of them cause symptoms [@bhatt2013global]. <!-- src: Bhatt et al. 2013, Nature 496:504–507, abstract: "390 million (95% credible interval 284-528) dengue infections per year" and "of which 96 million (67-136) manifest apparently"; https://doi.org/10.1038/nature12060 (abstract read via Europe PMC, PMID 23563266, https://europepmc.org/article/MED/23563266), accessed 2026-11-21 --> Cases reported to the World Health Organization reached a record of more than 14.6 million in 2024, with more than 12,000 deaths [@who2025dengue]. <!-- src: WHO fact sheet "Dengue and severe dengue" (21 August 2025), Global burden section, on 2024: "historic high of over 14.6 million cases and more than 12 000 dengue-related deaths"; https://www.who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/dengue-and-severe-dengue, accessed 2026-11-21 --> Peru had the largest dengue outbreak on its record in 2023 [@munayco2024peru], and Brazil its largest epidemic to date in 2024 [@gurgelgoncalves2024brazil]. Short-term forecasts for small areas can help health services time vector control and prepare clinical care. Dengue forecasts that give a range of likely values, rather than a single number, are well established [@johansson2019challenge; @lowe2016elife; @colongonzalez2021vietnam; @araujo2026sprint].

Dengue transmission depends partly on climate and the environment [@lowe2016elife; @colongonzalez2021vietnam] and on human movement [@harish2024movement]. Forecasters therefore often add descriptors of place from separate data products. Geospatial foundation models are trained on large archives of satellite data and other observations. For any location, they compress these observations into a fixed-length list of numbers, called an embedding. AlphaEarth Foundations provides a 64-number embedding for every 10 m pixel and every year from 2017 [@brown2025alphaearth; @aef_dataset]. It is computed from optical and radar satellite imagery by a model that was also trained to reconstruct climate, elevation and other layers. <!-- src: docs/research/literature.md verification round 2 item 4 (V1 ANNUAL, unit-length, 2017 onward); docs/research/data_and_tools.md AlphaEarth row (bands A00–A63, 10 m); inputs vs training targets: Brown et al. arXiv:2507.22291v2 Table S1 and section S15, read 2026-11-18 --> The Population Dynamics Foundation Model (PDFM) provides place embeddings built from search, map and activity data [@agarwal2024pdfm]. Even generic image features predict many outcomes [@rolf2021mosaiks; @lacoste2023geobench]. Because embeddings cover the whole globe, they are attractive where local data are sparse.

An embedding can also improve a forecast without telling the model anything about the environment. A district's embedding changes little from year to year, so a flexible model can use it to recognise the district and learn its usual dengue level. For example, it can learn that one district usually has more dengue than another without learning why. The embedding is then only a placeholder: a label that tells the model which district it is looking at. When every test district also appears in training, this labelling role alone can improve scores. We call this the identity confound: an apparent gain that comes from knowing which district is which. In spatial machine learning, coordinates and similar stand-ins for place help on places a model has seen but work poorly on new ones [@meyer2019spatial; @mila2024proxies]. In England, much of the gain from satellite-image embeddings for house prices, but not for air pollution, reflected coarse location [@metzler2026satellite].

The identity confound can be tested with location-only features and with places never seen in training. Dengue adds a complication. Our models see a district's cases in recent weeks and in the same weeks of the previous three years. These show roughly its usual dengue level, which is much of what a label could tell a model. Holding districts out of training stops a model from memorising which embedding belongs to which district. When it forecasts for a held-out district, however, it still receives that district's own recent case counts. The cleanest test of whether embeddings carry environmental information or merely identify a district therefore needs a model without case history, which cannot read a district's usual level from its counts. It also needs held-out places, where the model cannot have memorised the district.

Published dengue evaluations of place embeddings have not yet applied these tests. Brittain et al. added one PDFM snapshot to monthly forecasts for 5,570 Brazilian municipalities as a residual adjustment, with temporal hold-outs only [@brittain2026foundation]. In a retrospective study, we tested whether AlphaEarth embeddings improve weekly dengue forecasts in 6,909 scored second-level administrative units of Brazil, Colombia, Peru and Taiwan. <!-- src: S7_embedding_distance.csv n_units BR_S5 5,439 + CO_S5 1,070 + PE_S5 115 + TW_S5 285 = 6,909; Table 1 note explains the 6,990 reporting and 6,987 mapped districts --> These are municipalities in Brazil and Colombia, provinces in Peru and townships in Taiwan; we call them all districts. A location control is an input that tells the model where a district is, which district it is, or how much dengue it usually has, without describing its own environment directly.

Our main question was whether the embeddings improve forecasts. Location controls and analyses without case history bear on a second question, whether a gain reflects the environment or only district recognition, but cannot fully settle it. We turned these into four questions. When the test covers only future years, do embeddings reduce forecast error beyond the other inputs? Is any gain left once a location control is present? Does any gain appear in districts, states or countries left out of training, although their recent case counts remain inputs? Finally, in exploratory analyses, do embeddings predict a district's average dengue burden, and without case history, do they add anything beyond location?
