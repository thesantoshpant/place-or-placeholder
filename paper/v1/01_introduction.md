## 1 Introduction

Dengue causes a large burden of illness in the tropics and subtropics. [[GBD: authors to pull from IHME]] [@gbd2023diseases; @ihme2023gbdresults] Recent seasons in our study countries were exceptional: Peru had the largest dengue outbreak on its record in 2023 [@munayco2024peru], and Brazil its greatest epidemic to date in 2024 [@gurgelgoncalves2024brazil]. Short-term forecasts for small areas can help time vector control and prepare clinical services, and probabilistic dengue forecasting is well established [@johansson2019challenge; @lowe2016elife; @colongonzalez2021vietnam; @araujo2026sprint].

Dengue spread depends partly on environment and human movement [@harish2024movement], so forecasters often add descriptors of place, each needing its own data product. Geospatial foundation models offer a shortcut: they compress large data archives into a fixed-length vector, an embedding, for any location. AlphaEarth Foundations provides a 64-dimensional embedding for every 10 m pixel and every year from 2017, computed from optical and radar satellite imagery by a model that was also trained to reconstruct climate, elevation and other layers [@brown2025alphaearth; @aef_dataset]. <!-- src: docs/research/literature.md verification round 2 item 4 (V1 ANNUAL, unit-length, 2017 onward); docs/research/data_and_tools.md AlphaEarth row (bands A00–A63, 10 m); inputs vs training targets: Brown et al. arXiv:2507.22291v2 Table S1 and section S15 (inputs Sentinel-2, Landsat 8/9, Sentinel-1; ERA5-Land, GLO-30 DEM, GEDI, GRACE, PALSAR-2, NLCD, text used as training targets only), read 2026-11-18; include in the human reference check --> The Population Dynamics Foundation Model (PDFM) provides place embeddings built from search, maps and activity data [@agarwal2024pdfm]. Such vectors exist everywhere, which makes them attractive where data are thin.

An embedding is also a fingerprint. A district's vector changes slowly across years, so a flexible model can use it to recognise the district (and, say, its usual incidence) without using what the vector says about the environment. When every test district also appears in training, as in a split by time, this identifier role alone can improve scores. We call this the identity confound. Related problems are known in spatial machine learning: spatial proxies such as coordinates raise apparent skill but extrapolate poorly [@meyer2019spatial; @mila2024proxies], random cross-validation overstates skill for spatial data [@roberts2017cv; @ploton2020spatial], and in England much of the gain from satellite-image embeddings for house prices, though not for air pollution, reflected coarse location [@metzler2026satellite]. The identity confound can be tested with features that carry only location and with places never seen in training. Separately, lagging the embedding by one year keeps forecasts from using imagery of the year being forecast. For dengue there is a further complication: once a model sees each district's recent case counts, the case history already identifies the district. "Environmental information or identifier?" can then be answered cleanly only in models without case history.

Published dengue evaluations of place embeddings have not yet applied these tests. Brittain et al. added PDFM embeddings as a residual adjustment for monthly dengue in 5,570 Brazilian municipalities and reported lower weighted interval scores in 19 of 20 model-horizon combinations, using one October 2023 snapshot and temporal hold-outs only; we found no location-only control in their text [@brittain2026foundation]. <!-- src: docs/research/literature.md verification round 2 item 1 --> The Mexico dengue case study of Muslim et al. used a static snapshot; its gain at one month (a mean reduction in weighted interval score of 0.0051) was not significant at three or six months, and it had no location-only control or held-out region, although other case studies in that paper did [@muslim2026planetary]. <!-- src: docs/research/literature.md verification round 2 item 2 --> Nazir et al. reported AlphaEarth gains for malaria and respiratory infection without a coordinate, identifier or shuffled-embedding control or a spatial hold-out [@nazir2026alphaearth; @nazir2026malaria]. The closest precedent is a single-city study in India, where a satellite-embedding advantage over open map layers "fell to 0.001 once the open layers were given the cell's own coordinates" [@pradhan2026benchmarks]. Our study is not a replication of the PDFM work: we use embeddings computed from satellite imagery, lagged by one year, with weekly targets and a gradient-boosted forecaster.

We evaluate, retrospectively, whether AlphaEarth embeddings add skill to weekly forecasts beyond surveillance history and location in 6,987 second-level administrative units of Brazil, Colombia, Peru and Taiwan (6,909 scored in the test years) <!-- src: docs/data/boundaries_join.md (6,987 units with geometry); S7_embedding_distance.csv n_units BR_S5 5,439 + CO_S5 1,070 + PE_S5 115 + TW_S5 285 = 6,909 after the Espírito Santo exclusion --> (municipalities in Brazil and Colombia, provinces in Peru, townships in Taiwan; we call all of them districts). Our contributions are:

1. **A controlled comparison.** A ladder of quantile gradient-boosting models adds case history, climate, remote sensing and population, then the previous year's embeddings, with fixed rows and one configuration per country.
2. **Location-only controls and an excess-gain estimand.** Controls giving the model a district identifier, its position or its pre-study dengue level are added with and without embeddings. The excess gain, what embeddings add once such a control is present, is tested for difference and for equivalence within ±1%.
3. **Splits that remove the identifier route step by step:** new years, then held-out groups of states, buffered spatial blocks, random districts and whole countries. The 14 country-by-split cells form one family for multiplicity correction, fixed before any spatial-split result was seen. <!-- src: docs/plans/deviations_from_protocol.md row 12 (fcc3c38, before any spatial result) -->
4. **An exploratory arm without case history,** where an identifier and place information can in principle be told apart.

We ask four questions. **RQ1:** when only the years are new, do embeddings reduce forecast error beyond the non-embedding features? **RQ2:** do location-only controls add skill, and is any embedding gain left beyond them? **RQ3:** does any gain appear in districts, states or countries never seen in training? **RQ4 (exploratory):** in which weeks and districts do embeddings help or hurt? The share of an embedding gain that a control reproduces needs a clear gain to divide; with case history there is none, so it is examined only in the arm without case history.
