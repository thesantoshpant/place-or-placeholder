### 4.1 Principal findings

With case history in the model, AlphaEarth embeddings did not detectably improve weekly district dengue forecasts in any of the 14 comparisons, with or without a location control. <!-- src: T1_primary_family.csv pct_of_WIS_ref, Holm_p (m = 14), G_emb_pct, G_emb_lo95_pct, G_emb_hi95_pct --> Of six losses after correction, only the small one in Colombia's held-out states held up under both stricter checks and the "no control" rule; the others, including the loss with Taiwan held out (absolute 0.0037), depended on the control or the check. <!-- src: T1_primary_family.csv B4_Holm_p, p_iaaft_Holm (both < 0.05 only CO_S2; BR_S5 B4 0.095); BR_S5 G_emb_p 0.39; TW_S5 G_excess −0.00369; R3_A2_nocontrol_rule.csv CO_S2 Holm_p 0.0009; R3_C_seeds.csv CO_S2 -->

In unseen states, the embeddings predicted which districts carry a high dengue burden, less well than standard covariates but far better than coordinates; in Brazil and Colombia they added little once both were in the model, even without case history (Section 3.9). <!-- src: R3_E1_info_probe.md T1 LightGBM grouped-state CV: E 0.49/0.52/0.24/0.45; S 0.65/0.66/0.43/0.55; ΔR² E over G+S BR +0.010, CO +0.010; PE −0.071 and TW +0.070 with CIs including zero -->

At one and four weeks ahead, recent cases carry nearly all the forecasting skill, which leaves little for any non-case input, embeddings included, to add. The design detected known covariates only in Brazil and partly in Colombia (Section 3.1), so the null results in Peru and Taiwan say little. <!-- src: R3_A1_positive_control.csv BR_S1 L1->L2 0.57, L2->L3 0.52; CO_S1 L2->L3 0.30 -->

The earlier dengue evaluations of place embeddings that we found used neither location-only controls nor held-out places [@brittain2026foundation; @muslim2026planetary]. We applied both across four countries, extending a coordinate control used in one Indian city [@pradhan2026benchmarks]. Equivalence tests ruled out effects larger than 1% of forecast error in 8 comparisons under the main test, and in 5 of these under both stricter checks. <!-- src: T1b_margin_sensitivity.csv margin 1.0 within_Holm (8/14); Introduction paragraph 5 and docs/research/literature.md verification round 2 items 1 and 2 (Brittain: temporal hold-outs only, no location-only control found; Muslim Mexico dengue case study: no location-only control or held-out region); literature.md section 2 (Pradhan: one city, coordinates, no spatial transfer); R3_A4_equivalence.csv G_excess margin 1.0, equivalent_Holm True under both EWC (primary) and EWC B=4 for BR_S2, BR_S3, CO_S1, CO_S2, CO_S4; R3_A8_iaaft_equivalence.csv equivalent_1pct_calibrated True for the same five (calibrated 90% CI, not Holm-adjusted; Section 3.3 says so) -->

### 4.2 Why the embeddings can make forecasts worse

Most rows are weeks filled with zero, and the losses concentrate there. In Colombia, however, the loss persisted in district-years with reported cases (Table S48), so it is not only an artefact of filled zeros. <!-- src: R3_A6_outcome_agnostic.csv CO_S1, CO_S2, CO_S4 case district-years p < 0.01 (max 0.0084); Results 3.5 --> We did not analyse how the trees use the embeddings; the following are hypotheses. Annual embeddings do not change within a season, so one possibility is that the model uses them mainly to move its forecasts up or down. They did raise the upper forecast quantiles on zero weeks everywhere (Section 3.5). <!-- src: S6_q90_mechanism.csv dq90_log > 0 in 14/14; R3_A6_outcome_agnostic.csv zero-week width 14/14 (significant in 13), case-week coverage PE_S5 +2.1 pp (week-weighted, Table S48) --> Such a shift costs accuracy on zero weeks and gains it on case weeks, whether or not it helps overall (Section 2.8).

Part of the loss may come simply from adding many columns. In Colombia, Peru and Taiwan, shuffled embeddings of the same size cost one-third to four-fifths as much as the real ones; in Brazil they cost almost nothing, while the real ones gained slightly (Section 3.6). Compact summaries also shrank the loss (Section 3.9). <!-- src: S2_control_panel.csv C3 G_id_pct / G_emb_pct: colombia 0.34, peru 0.81; R3_C_tw2021_panel.csv taiwan 0.58/1.06 = 0.55; R3_C_variants.csv V1, V2 --> Sixty-four columns that barely change within a district may give the trees many weak, correlated inputs to choose between, whatever the columns contain. AlphaEarth was trained partly to reconstruct climate reanalysis and elevation [@brown2025alphaearth], which may explain why the embeddings repeat information already in the climate and remote-sensing inputs. <!-- src: R3_C_variants.csv V3, V4; Brown et al. arXiv:2507.22291v2 Table S1 (ERA5-Land and GLO-30 DEM as training targets), read 2026-11-18 -->

Tree size also matters. In Brazil, tuning each model separately changed WIS by about eight times the embedding gain (Supplementary Methods, section C), so comparisons that tune each input set separately partly measure the tuning. <!-- src: S1 section C (T2_ladder_long.csv: 0.00327/0.00043 = 7.7 pooled h{1,4}); R3_A5_planned_vs_reported.csv PE_S1 per-level +0.65 --> Tuning does not explain the Colombian loss (Section 3.9). <!-- src: R3_C_tuning.csv -->

The comparison with Taiwan held out was a stress test. With 98% of its rows zero-filled, the reference error was very low, so a small absolute loss became a large percentage. Its embeddings also lay far from those of the training districts, so the model had to extrapolate. <!-- src: T5_strata.csv TW_S5 zero_filled row_share 0.98; S7b_embedding_distance_bins.csv TW_S5 cos_max_mean 0.70–0.82; S6_q90_mechanism.csv TW_S5 dq90_log 0.0929 --> Every Taiwanese district had a loss, whatever its similarity to the training districts [@meyer2021aoa], mostly in district-years without cases. <!-- src: S7b_embedding_distance_bins.csv TW_S5 G_excess_share_units_neg = 1; R3_A6_outcome_agnostic.csv TW_S5 −3.28 on case district-years vs −18.3 -->

One possible explanation is that embeddings differ systematically between countries, so a model can use them to learn each country's case definition and reporting level. Taiwan counts only locally acquired confirmed cases, so a level learned from the other countries would be wrong for every Taiwanese district. This fits the embedding gain better than the excess gain, because both excess-gain models here contain shuffled embeddings, which already mark the country. We did not test embeddings centred within each country, which would block this route. Dengue is also not endemic in Taiwan: imported cases can start local epidemics, and local cases are concentrated in Tainan, Kaohsiung and Pingtung in the south [@shang2010imported]. <!-- src: Shang et al. 2010, PLoS Negl Trop Dis 4(8):e775: "non-endemic areas such as Taiwan" (Discussion); "imported dengue cases are able to initiate indigenous epidemics" (Abstract, Conclusions); "98.45% (9,756/9,910) of total confirmed indigenous cases in Taiwan" were in Tainan, Kaohsiung and Pingtung, 1998–2007 (Results); https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pntd.0000775 (full text read via Europe PMC, PMC2914757), accessed 2026-11-21 --> Environmental similarity to endemic districts therefore says little about local risk.

### 4.3 Forecasts without case history

In these exploratory analyses without case history, coordinates and a district label both reduced error in Brazil's future years, and the embeddings added nothing detectable beyond either. In that setting, the embeddings may have worked partly as a placeholder, telling the model where a district was or which district it was. In held-out states, where a label cannot help, nothing was detected, but intervals were wide. <!-- src: T6b_nohist_estimands.csv BR_S1_nohist pooled G_id 3.30, G_excess −0.43; docs/results/review/RH_nohist_c6.md (C6 G_id +1.98; G_excess with C6 −0.04); BR_S2_nohist pooled G_id +0.88 [−0.48, 2.23], G_emb +1.52 [−1.27, 4.32], G_excess −0.01 [−1.28, 1.27], mde_pct 1.80 --> The labelling pattern we had specified in advance (Section 3.8) appeared in neither split. <!-- src: docs/methods/nohist_arm.md "Interpretation"; FINAL_RESULTS_BRIEF.md section 4 --> Other results argue against a pure labelling role. Shuffled embeddings, which identify districts as stably as the real ones, did not help in Brazil's future years (Table S36) or at the 2019 selection without case history (Table S22). In Brazil, a district's embedding also changes from year to year about as much as it differs from its neighbours (Table S49). <!-- src: S2_control_panel.csv brazil C3 G_id −0.04; T4_cstar_selection.csv BR_S1_nohist N3 0.35428 vs C3 0.35963; R3_A7_embedding_stability.csv BR 0.982 vs 0.974 --> <!-- src: (Colombia sentence cut in v1g; Section 3.8) R3_C_co_s2_nohist.csv --> Coordinates also follow climate zones, which makes a placeholder role hard to separate from the environment. These analyses used one seed and settings tuned with case history, and they included no shuffled-embedding run in the test years.

### 4.4 Comparison with other studies

Our results do not contradict the PDFM studies, which used another embedding, monthly targets and a residual design [@brittain2026foundation; @muslim2026planetary], but small gains under temporal hold-outs need a location-only control. Like Pradhan [@pradhan2026benchmarks], we found no detectable embedding advantage, with or without location inputs. In Brazil the point estimate moved from +0.29% without a control to +0.02% with coordinates (seed 0; Table S36), well within the uncertainty. <!-- src: S2_control_panel.csv brazil G_emb_pct 0.2858 (seed 0; Table S36 row "none (embedding gain, L3 vs L4)"), C2 G_excess_pct 0.0196 (Table S36 row C2) --> AlphaEarth gains for malaria and respiratory infection [@nazir2026alphaearth; @nazir2026malaria] come from settings without weekly case histories.

### 4.5 Strengths and limitations

The study's strengths are its four countries, several location controls, a positive control, equivalence tests and a dated log of protocol changes. We could not test PDFM, the embedding used in both earlier dengue studies, because it requires approved access [@agarwal2024pdfm]; our results apply to AlphaEarth only.

When the difference between models drifted across seasons, the main test found effects too often. In five comparisons, the surrogate-series check showed that the main test, set at 5%, rejected 14% to 18% of the time (Table S47; earlier simulations in Table S12). <!-- src: R3_A8_iaaft_equivalence.csv size_nominal5, method ewc_hln: BR_S1 18.4, BR_S2 14.0, BR_S3 15.2, BR_S4 14.9, CO_S5 15.8 (Table S47); Table S12 IAAFT worst comparisons 0.10–0.22 --> In the loss comparisons we checked, the rate was close to the intended level (1% to 9%). <!-- src: R3_A8_iaaft_equivalence.csv BR_S5 6.5, CO_S1 8.7, CO_S2 1.1, CO_S4 7.35 --> The inflated rates weaken the equivalence results in those five comparisons. They do not weaken the finding of no gain: a test that rejects too often finds gains too easily, and the main test still found none.

Every model used the same LightGBM settings, tuned without embeddings over a small grid. This removes tuning as a confounder but may not suit 64 extra correlated inputs. In Brazil, separate tuning favoured smaller trees, most strongly for the embedding model. Both models were re-tuned only in Colombia's future years, and the held-out-country settings, Taiwan's included, were not tuned for pooled data. <!-- src: S1_supplement_methods.md section C "Per-level tuning" (L4 gap 1.36%) and "Tuning" (S5 settings); Table S43 PE_S1 per-level +0.65; Table S55 --> The embeddings also entered as 64 raw inputs. They are designed for linear and nearest-neighbour use, and because their axes are arbitrary, tree splits may use them inefficiently. A nearest-neighbour risk feature and eight principal components, which suit that design better, also gave no gain (Section 3.9). Other learners, residual designs and learned location encoders [@russwurm2024location; @vivanco2023geoclip; @klemmer2025satclip] may behave differently.

We used one snapshot of final, revised counts. In practice the latest weeks are incomplete when a forecast is made, so all models would be less accurate in real time, and place inputs might matter more under delayed reporting. Shifting the case inputs back by two weeks in Brazil's future years gave an embedding gain of +0.60% (95% CI −0.56 to 1.76; Table S37), but this does not reproduce real reporting delays. <!-- src: T6a_sensitivities.csv Brazil S1 delay2 G_emb (Table S37: +0.60, −0.56 to 1.76, p 0.29) --> Test years 2020–2021 fell in the COVID-19 pandemic, when dengue notification declined in parts of Latin America [@chen2022covid; @roster2024covidbrazil]. AlphaEarth was trained on imagery that overlaps our test years [@brown2025alphaearth], which, if anything, favours the embeddings. <!-- src: R8_literature.md section 4 (Brown et al. §S15, §S22.2); R10_fact_check.md row 2 -->

Filled zeros were checked against independent district-level data only in Brazil and Taiwan, so some in Colombia and Peru may hide unreported cases. If they do, the losses may be overstated: the embedding model gave higher upper forecasts on these weeks (Section 3.5), and these would then score better. The hidden-case check, which changed only the scored weeks and not the models' inputs or training targets, left only two of the six losses significant after correction in every draw at rates like Brazil's (Table S56). <!-- src: S6_q90_mechanism.csv dq90_log > 0 in 14/14 (Table S31); R3_A6_outcome_agnostic.csv zero-week width (Table S48). Reasoning, not a result: with a true count above zero, higher upper quantiles lower the pinball loss. Hidden cases in the case-history inputs and training targets of both models are not covered. R5_zero_fill_stress.csv s_pct 2.1, k 1 and 3: exc_share_holm_sig_loss = 1 only for BR_S5 and CO_S2 (Table S56). --> In Espírito Santo, the false zeros created by our zero-filling were removed from scoring but remain in the training targets. <!-- src: docs/plans/deviations_from_protocol.md row 34; S1_supplement_methods.md A4, A6 -->

Because training began in 2018, inputs that are constant within a year had few district-years to learn from in early test years. Held-out districts were only partly unseen (Section 2.7). Had we tuned tree size within each fold, we would have chosen smaller trees in 12 of 15 Brazilian folds. <!-- src: RF_summary.md --> Removing all case history does not mimic sparse or delayed surveillance.

Our conclusions apply only to the seasons we studied. With Peru or Taiwan held out, only the excess gain was averaged over three random seeds. <!-- src: R3_C_summary.md section 3 --> Some decisions, including the ±1% margin, one set of settings per country and the inclusion of Taiwan's 2021 season, followed results (Section 2.12). Further limitations are in Supplementary Methods, section I.

### 4.6 Implications and future work

For a dengue programme, the largest loss within a country means forecast case counts off by roughly an extra 0.2% (Section 3.2), whereas case history cut forecast error by about 60% relative to the seasonal naive forecast (Section 3.1). <!-- src: Section 3.2 (T1 G_excess PE_S1 −0.00197; exp(0.002) − 1 ≈ 0.2%); S1_baselines.csv rel_wis_vs_L0 L1 0.362–0.425 (Section 3.1: 0.36 to 0.43 times; Table S17) --> We did not evaluate vector-control or clinical decisions, but differences this small seem unlikely to change them. A programme whose weekly forecasts already use recent case counts should not expect much from adding AlphaEarth embeddings. Small gains cannot be ruled out: the upper ends of the 95% CIs reach +0.79% in Brazil's future years, +0.71% in its held-out districts and +2.02% with Peru held out. <!-- src: T1_primary_family.csv BR_S1, BR_S4, PE_S5 hi95_pct -->

For outbreak preparedness, forecast ranges probably matter more than these small differences. Every model was overconfident in weeks with cases: the full model's 80% intervals contained only 61% to 75% of those weeks (23% in Taiwan's future years). <!-- src: Table S20 cov_80_nonzero L4 + C*; Table S18; S1_supplement_methods.md section I (other ladder steps 56–75%) --> A team planning from these ranges would see counts fall outside them more often than the one week in five that an 80% range allows. Embeddings did not fix this, and recalibration helped mainly in Peru (Table S34). <!-- src: S5_recalibration.csv cov80pos_L4+C* (Peru raw 0.67–0.70 → 0.71–0.81 across recalibration variants; Brazil about +0.03; Colombia and Taiwan about 0 or lower); S1_supplement_methods.md section I "Calibration"; Table S34 -->

Follow-up work will test PDFM in Brazil, a second model class, embeddings centred within each country, and settings with reporting delays and longer horizons.

### 4.7 Conclusions

Across four countries, AlphaEarth embeddings carried information about where dengue burden is high, but in Brazil and Colombia standard climate, remote-sensing and population inputs already held most of it. With each district's recent case counts in the model, the embeddings did not detectably improve weekly district dengue forecasts. Under the main test, effects larger than ±1% were ruled out in 8 comparisons, and in 5 of these under both stricter checks as well. That margin is about Brazil's combined gain from all non-case inputs. <!-- src: T1b_margin_sensitivity.csv margin 1.0 within_Holm (8/14); R3_A4_equivalence.csv G_excess margin 1.0: EWC B=4 equivalent_Holm True for BR_S2, BR_S3, CO_S1, CO_S2, CO_S4 (5/14), and EWC (primary) equivalent_Holm True for the same five; R3_A8_iaaft_equivalence.csv equivalent_1pct_calibrated True for the same five (surrogate-calibrated 90% CI, not Holm-adjusted; stated in Section 3.3); Table S47 letters "a b c d" and last column "yes" --> With Taiwan held out, whose case definition differs from that of the training countries, embeddings worsened forecasts, though not robustly. Without case history, our results do not settle whether the embeddings act only as a placeholder: the estimates were imprecise, and the pattern we had specified in advance did not appear (Section 4.3). A gain from foundation-model features shows useful environmental information only if it exceeds the gain from location-only controls in places left out of training. Such a test should use the same settings for every model and fix in advance an equivalence margin, the smallest difference that would matter.
