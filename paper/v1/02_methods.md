### 2.1 Study design

This retrospective forecasting study used one snapshot of final, revised data and followed EPIFORGE 2020 [@pollett2021epiforge] (Supplementary Methods, section G). Figure 1 summarises the design. Automated AI agents wrote the code and ran the downloads, checks, model runs and analyses (see Use of AI tools). Codes such as D24 refer to the deviation log (section F), and the glossary is in section H. A forecast is made at an origin, the end of the last week whose data it may use; each combination of country and evaluation split is a cell.

### 2.3 Forecasting task

At each origin, a model forecasts the cases h weeks ahead (the horizon) as nine quantiles of log(1 + cases): the deciles 0.1 to 0.9, including the median. Horizons were 1 to 4 weeks in the new-years split and 1 and 4 weeks elsewhere, bracketing the one to four weeks used by forecast hubs [@cramer2022covidhub]. <!-- src: docs/methods/splits.md "Origins"; docs/methods/runner.md --> For each test year Y, one model per horizon was trained on targets from the epidemiological years 2018 to Y − 1, the same rows for every model. (An epidemiological year is the calendar year of a week's Wednesday.) AlphaEarth starts in 2017 and serves 2018 targets, so the 2020 models learned from two years and the 2024 models from six. <!-- src: docs/methods/runner.md; docs/plans/deviations_from_protocol.md row 4 -->

### 2.4 Models and inputs

The models form a ladder, each step adding one block of inputs (Figure 1A). The seasonal naive baseline (L0) repeats the cases of the same week a year earlier. The case-history model (L1) uses cases in the 12 weeks to the origin, the same weeks in the previous three years and the week of the year. The climate step (L2) adds population-weighted ERA5-Land temperature, precipitation and humidity, lagged up to 12 weeks [@era5land_ee]. The remote-sensing and population step (L3) adds MODIS vegetation greenness (NDVI) and land-surface temperature for the last three complete months [@mod13q1_ee; @mod11a2_ee], and night-time lights, built-up share and log population for the year before the target year [@viirs_ee; @ghsl_built; @ghsl_pop]. This model, with every input except the embeddings, is the reference model. The embedding step (L4) adds the 64-number AlphaEarth embedding of the year before the target year [@brown2025alphaearth; @aef_dataset], as a population-weighted district mean rescaled to unit length. Table S5 reports an independent recomputation of these means and Table S6 lists every input. <!-- src: docs/methods/runner.md "Features" (Brazil p = 1, 23, 74, 86, 150); docs/features/alphaearth_verification.md; S1_supplement_methods.md A7 (Fernando de Noronha) -->

The forecasting model was LightGBM quantile regression [@ke2017lightgbm], one tree ensemble per quantile and horizon, with learning rate 0.05 and a fixed 400 rounds (no early stopping). The seed sets row and column subsampling and the random draw of the shuffled-embedding and random-field controls. <!-- src: S1_supplement_methods.md section C "LightGBM"; docs/methods/runner.md "Models and tuning" --> Each fit used all training rows of its split (for example, 1,439,937 rows for the Brazilian new-years reference model for 2023 at horizon 1). <!-- src: docs/methods/compute_plan.md (Brazil S1, L3, test year 2023, h1: n_train = 1,439,937) --> Baselines were the seasonal naive forecast, a negative binomial regression and, in Brazil, the pretrained time-series model TimesFM 2.5 used without training on our data [@das2024timesfm; @timesfm25]. Each country used one LightGBM configuration for every model, control and split (D2). Tuned once on the case-history model (train 2018, validate 2019), it had 127 leaves per tree in Brazil and 31 elsewhere, with at least 100 rows per leaf; models trained across countries used Brazil's configuration, or Colombia's when Brazil was held out. <!-- src: docs/methods/split_pipeline.md "Params"; docs/methods/runner.md "Models and tuning" --> Tuning each model separately, as in the original protocol, is a sensitivity analysis. All runs used [pending] core-hours on one 64-thread server. <!-- PENDING: total core-hours from the scheduler logs (EXP-B, round3_runs.md) -->

### 2.5 Location controls

A location control tells the model where a district is, or its past dengue level, without describing the ground (Table S7). <!-- src: docs/methods/controls.md --> Historical risk (C1) is the district's mean log(1 + cases) in the years before 2018 (2014–2017 in Brazil). A held-out district receives the distance-weighted mean of its five nearest training districts; across countries it is undefined. <!-- src: src/pop/features/controls.py c1_source_rows; docs/methods/controls.md --> Coordinates (C2) are latitude, longitude and 80 spherical harmonics of degree up to 8 [@russwurm2024location]; the harmonics vary over thousands of kilometres, so within Peru and Taiwan latitude and longitude carry most of the information. Random fields are 64 random smooth maps at the embeddings' spatial range (C4; 8,483 km in Brazil, close to national trends) or at the buffer distance of the held-out-regions split (C4s; 225 km in Brazil, 130 km in Colombia). <!-- src: S1_supplement_methods.md Table S7 (C2: degree 1–8); docs/methods/controls.md table and C4s rule; docs/plans/deviations_from_protocol.md row 15 --> The shuffled embedding (C3) randomly reassigns, within each country, every district's real AlphaEarth series to another district, fixed across years; a few districts keep their own. The identifier (C6) is the district ID as one categorical input, in the new-years split only. A 2023 snapshot embedding (C5) was never selected. <!-- src: src/pop/features/controls.py _perm (rejects only the identity permutation); RC_repro_audit.md M3 -->

With case history in the model, no control tests identity, because the case history already identifies every district, held out or not; the identity question is addressed only without case history (Section 2.11). Within a country, a control acts as a geographic or historical-risk prior for held-out districts. With a whole country held out, the shuffled embedding carries no information about the test districts: it is a dimension-matched noise control, measuring what 64 extra columns cost regardless of content.

One control per cell, the selected control, was chosen on 2019 data (rule v2, D19): among controls within two standard errors of the difference from the best 2019 score (seed standard deviation pooled over multi-seed controls), we took the first in the order historical risk, shuffled embedding, coordinates, short-range random fields, random fields and snapshot. <!-- src: scripts/run/select_control.py docstring; RC_repro_audit.md M4 --> The order puts first a district's own dengue level, then the control matched to the embeddings in size, then smooth geography. No control was not a candidate; a sensitivity rule added after review sets the control to none unless one beat the reference model at 2019 by more than two standard errors, making the excess gain equal the embedding gain. A fixed panel in the new-years split substitutes four controls in turn.

### 2.6 Evaluation splits

Five splits define the training and test sets (Figure 1B). New years (S1) uses all districts and tests on later years. Held-out states (S2) holds out whole states (departments in Colombia) in five folds. Held-out regions (S3) holds out compact blocks of neighbouring districts and drops training districts within a buffer distance [@roberts2017cv; @ploton2020spatial]. The buffer is the distance at which correlations of deseasonalised 2014–2019 case residuals first fell below 0.1 (225 km in Brazil, 130 km in Colombia; Supplementary Methods, section D3). Held-out districts (S4) assigns districts at random to five folds within states; held-out country (S5) trains on three countries and tests on the fourth. <!-- src: docs/methods/splits.md "Splits" (k-means blocks on centroids) and "Buffer distance D"; RC_repro_audit.md M6 -->

S2 to S4 were run only in Brazil and Colombia (Peru's 115 provinces are too few), giving 14 cells. <!-- src: docs/data/panel_weekly_v1.md (Peru 115 units); T1_primary_family.csv (14 cells: BR S1–S5, CO S1–S5, PE S1/S5, TW S1/S5) --> Held-out districts were never in a training fit, so the model could not memorise which embedding belongs to which of them, but they kept their own lagged cases as inputs: held out means never trained on, not without surveillance history.

### 2.8 Scoring

The primary score was the weighted interval score (WIS) [@bracher2021wis], a proper scoring rule [@gneiting2007proper], on log(1 + cases) [@bosse2023transformed]. The WIS rewards prediction intervals that are narrow yet contain the observation; lower is better, and for a single-value forecast it equals the absolute error. For an observation $y$ and a central interval from $l$ to $u$ with coverage $1-\alpha$, the interval score and the WIS are

$$\begin{aligned}
\mathrm{IS}_{\alpha}(l, u; y) &= (u - l) + \tfrac{2}{\alpha}\,(l - y)_{+} + \tfrac{2}{\alpha}\,(y - u)_{+},\\
\mathrm{WIS}(F, y) &= \frac{1}{K + 1/2}\Big(\tfrac{1}{2}\,\lvert y - m\rvert + \sum_{k=1}^{K} \tfrac{\alpha_k}{2}\,\mathrm{IS}_{\alpha_k}(l_k, u_k; y)\Big),
\end{aligned} \qquad (1)$$

where $F$ is the forecast, $y$ is the observed log(1 + cases), $m$ is the forecast median, and $(x)_{+}$ equals $x$ when $x$ is positive and 0 otherwise. Our nine quantiles give the median plus $K = 4$ central intervals, with bounds $l_k$ and $u_k$ and $\alpha_k$ = 0.8, 0.6, 0.4 and 0.2 for coverage 20%, 40%, 60% and 80%. Our WIS values are therefore not comparable with the 23-quantile WIS of forecast hubs.

Secondary measures were the WIS on the case scale and the coverage of the 40% and 80% intervals. We split rows into weeks with at least one case, zeros listed by the source (Brazil 2020–2021 only) and zero-filled weeks. These strata depend on the outcome and are descriptive only: a forecast that widens its upper tail will tend to lose on zero weeks and gain on case weeks whether or not it is better overall, the forecaster's dilemma [@lerch2017dilemma]. We therefore also report measures that do not condition on the week's outcome.

### 2.9 Gains

We write $\overline{W}_M$ for the mean WIS of model $M$ on the shared rows, with each target week weighted equally (Section 2.10). Where a table gives a plain mean over rows instead, we say so. A gain is positive when the second model forecasts better. The embedding gain is how much the embeddings improve the reference model:

$$\Delta_{\text{emb}} = \overline{W}_{R} - \overline{W}_{R+E} \qquad (2)$$

where $R$ is the reference model (L3) and $R+E$ is the same model with the embeddings added (L4). The control gain is how much the selected location control alone improves the reference model:

$$\Delta_{\text{ctrl}} = \overline{W}_{R} - \overline{W}_{R+C} \qquad (3)$$

where $R+C$ is the reference model with the selected control (L3 + C\*). The excess gain is what the embeddings add once the model already has the selected control:

$$\Delta_{\text{excess}} = \overline{W}_{R+C} - \overline{W}_{R+C+E} \qquad (4)$$

where $R+C+E$ has both the control and the embeddings (L4 + C\*).

The excess gain was the protocol's primary measure. After review we made the embedding gain co-primary, because the selected control sometimes harmed the reference model (Section 3.3), and the excess gain then measures the embeddings inside a weakened model. Gains are reported as percentages of $\overline{W}_R$ and as absolute differences, because $\overline{W}_R$ differs widely between countries. The identity share, $\Delta_{\text{ctrl}}/\Delta_{\text{emb}}$, was to be reported only if the embedding gain's 95% confidence interval (CI) excluded zero and the gain was at least 1% of $\overline{W}_R$. <!-- src: docs/methods/eval_library.md estimands.py; RESOLUTION_LOG.md section B --> Code and result files keep the earlier name "identity gain" (G_id) for the control gain.

### 2.10 Statistical analysis

For each target week we averaged the WIS difference over districts and then over horizons 1 and 4; the mean of this weekly series is the estimate. <!-- src: src/pop/eval/estimands.py infer_estimand --> Because errors in neighbouring weeks are correlated, we used the Diebold–Mariano test [@diebold1995comparing] with the equal-weighted cosine (EWC) variance estimator for correlated series [@lazarus2018har] and the Harvey–Leybourne–Newbold small-sample correction [@harvey1997testing]. <!-- src: src/pop/eval/stats.py ewc_inference; T1_primary_family.csv df (BR 16, CO 11, PE 13, TW 16) --> Weeks are the sampling unit, so inference concerns these districts and seasons, not other seasons, and it treats each fitted model as fixed, omitting variation between random seeds.

Each co-primary measure forms a family of 14 cells, with Holm's correction for multiple testing within the family [@holm1979simple]. Only the excess-gain family was fixed before the held-out-place results (D12). Because Brazil held-out districts is still running, Holm values are over the 13 completed cells; for any result in the 14th cell, every loss reported here keeps a Holm-adjusted p below 0.05 (largest 0.029). <!-- src: computed from T1_primary_family.csv DM_p with a 14th p value set to 1 (worst case): CO_S3 0.0294, CO_S4 0.0282, TW_S5 0.0185, CO_S1 0.0132, CO_S2 0.0073, BR_S5 0.0010; with p = 0 the values equal Holm_p_available --> <!-- PENDING: final Holm m=14 -->

We also tested equivalence with two one-sided tests (TOST) [@lakens2017equivalence]: a cell was equivalent when its 90% CI lay inside ±1% of the reference model's WIS, unadjusted or Holm-adjusted. The 1% value was the original protocol's threshold for a material embedding gain. We chose it as the equivalence margin after the Brazil and Peru new-years results had been seen (D9). <!-- src: docs/plans/deviations_from_protocol.md row 9 ("Partly: Brazil S1 and Peru S1 results were already seen"); docs/plans/paper1_review_response.md (ID share only if G_emb ≥ 1% of WIS(L3)) --> For scale, we compare it with each new-years cell's gains from climate (L1 to L2) and from the whole non-case stack (L1 to L3), tested the same way.
