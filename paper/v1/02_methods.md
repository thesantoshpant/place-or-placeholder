### 2.1 Study design

This retrospective study used one snapshot of final, revised data and followed the EPIFORGE 2020 guideline [@pollett2021epiforge] (Supplementary Methods, section G; design in Figure 1). Automated AI agents wrote the code and ran the downloads, checks, model runs and analyses (see Use of AI tools). Codes for models, controls and splits are defined in the glossary (Supplementary Methods, section H). Each forecast is made at a forecast origin: the end of the last week whose data it may use. From the end of week 6, for example, the forecasts one and four weeks ahead are for weeks 7 and 10. We call one country with one evaluation split a comparison.

### 2.3 Forecasting task

At each origin, a model forecasts the cases h weeks ahead (the horizon). The forecast is a set of nine quantiles of log(1 + cases), the deciles 0.1 to 0.9; these give a median and ranges of likely values. Horizons were 1 to 4 weeks in the future-years split and 1 and 4 weeks elsewhere, as in forecast hubs [@cramer2022covidhub]. <!-- src: docs/methods/splits.md "Origins"; docs/methods/runner.md --> A week's epidemiological year is the calendar year of its Wednesday. For each test year Y, one model per horizon was trained on targets from epidemiological years 2018 to Y − 1, the same rows for every model. Because AlphaEarth begins in 2017 and each embedding serves the next year, training starts in 2018. The 2020 models therefore learned from two years of data and the 2024 models from six. <!-- src: docs/methods/runner.md; docs/plans/deviations_from_protocol.md row 4 -->

### 2.4 Models and inputs

We built a ladder of models, each step adding one block of inputs (Figure 1A; Table S6): recent cases (L1), climate (L2), remote sensing and population (L3, the reference model) and embeddings (L4, the embedding model). The seasonal naive baseline (L0) repeats the cases of the same week a year earlier. The case-history model (L1) uses cases in the 12 weeks to the origin, the same weeks in the previous three years and the week of the year. The climate step (L2) adds population-weighted ERA5-Land temperature, precipitation and humidity, lagged up to 12 weeks [@era5land_ee]. The remote-sensing and population step (L3) adds MODIS vegetation greenness (NDVI) and land-surface temperature for the last three complete months [@mod13q1_ee; @mod11a2_ee], and night-time lights, built-up share and log population for the year before the target year [@viirs_ee; @ghsl_built; @ghsl_pop]. The embedding model (L4) adds the 64-number AlphaEarth embedding for the year before the target year [@brown2025alphaearth; @aef_dataset]. It enters as a population-weighted district average rescaled to unit length (recomputed independently in Table S5). <!-- src: docs/methods/runner.md "Features" (Brazil p = 1, 23, 74, 86, 150); docs/features/alphaearth_verification.md; S1_supplement_methods.md A7 (Fernando de Noronha) -->

The forecasting model was LightGBM quantile regression [@ke2017lightgbm], which combines many small decision trees. We fitted one set of trees per quantile and horizon (learning rate 0.05; a fixed 400 rounds, no early stopping). A random seed sets which rows and inputs each tree samples and how the random controls are drawn. <!-- src: S1_supplement_methods.md section C "LightGBM"; docs/methods/runner.md "Models and tuning" --> Each fit used all training rows of its split (for example, 1,439,937 for the Brazilian future-years reference model, 2023, horizon 1). <!-- src: docs/methods/compute_plan.md (Brazil S1, L3, test year 2023, h1: n_train = 1,439,937) --> Baselines were the seasonal naive forecast, a negative binomial regression and, in Brazil, the pretrained time-series model TimesFM 2.5 without further training [@das2024timesfm; @timesfm25]. Each country used one set of LightGBM settings for every model, control and split, tuned once on the case-history model (train 2018, validate 2019). Within-country models had 127 leaves per tree in Brazil and 31 in Colombia, Peru and Taiwan, with at least 100 rows per leaf. Held-out-country models used Brazil's settings (127 leaves), or Colombia's when Brazil was held out. <!-- src: S1_supplement_methods.md section C "Tuning"; docs/methods/split_pipeline.md "Params"; docs/methods/runner.md "Models and tuning" --> The protocol planned to tune each model separately; we report that as a sensitivity analysis. All runs used about 4,700 core-hours on one 64-thread server. <!-- src: _compute_total.txt (4,662.1 core-hours over 6,180 completed chunks, all runs including superseded and sensitivity runs; invalidated chunks excluded) -->

### 2.5 Location controls

Location controls test whether location information alone could reproduce an apparent benefit of the embeddings. We used six candidate controls (Table S7): five location controls and a 2023 embedding snapshot. We call the control selected for a comparison C\*. <!-- src: docs/methods/controls.md -->

- **Average past cases (C1)**: the district's mean log(1 + cases) before 2018 (2014–2017 in Brazil). A held-out district gets the distance-weighted mean of its five nearest training districts. With a whole country held out, this control cannot be computed. <!-- src: src/pop/features/controls.py c1_source_rows; docs/methods/controls.md -->
- **Coordinates (C2)**: latitude, longitude and 80 smooth wave patterns over the globe, which help the model represent broad regional gradients (spherical harmonics up to degree 8) [@russwurm2024location].
- **Shuffled embeddings (C3)**: each district receives another district's AlphaEarth series from the same country, fixed across years (a few keep their own).
- **Smooth spatial noise (C4)**: 64 random smooth maps that vary over the same distance as the embeddings. A short-range version (C4s) varies over the buffer distance of the held-out-regions split. <!-- src: S1_supplement_methods.md Table S7 (C2: degree 1–8); docs/methods/controls.md table and C4s rule; docs/plans/deviations_from_protocol.md row 15 -->
- **District label (C6)**: the district's ID as one categorical input, usable only when the test covers future years. It was a candidate only in the analysis without case history; with case history it appears only in the fixed control panel (Table S36).

The 2023 snapshot (C5) was never selected. <!-- src: src/pop/features/controls.py _perm (rejects only the identity permutation); RC_repro_audit.md M3 -->

With a whole country held out, shuffled embeddings carry no district-level location. Because they are drawn within each country, they still mark the country. They then act mainly as a noise control, showing what 64 extra columns cost.

We chose each comparison's control on 2019 data. Controls whose 2019 score was within two standard errors of the best were treated as tied, and we took the first tied control in this order: average past cases, shuffled embeddings, coordinates, short-range noise, long-range noise and the snapshot. <!-- src: scripts/run/select_control.py docstring; RC_repro_audit.md M4 --> The rule always picks a control, even when none beats the reference model, because the excess gain needs one to compare against. A sensitivity rule added after review (the "no control" rule) picks none unless a control beats the reference model in 2019 by more than two standard errors; with no control, the excess gain equals the embedding gain. In the future-years split we also reran each comparison with each of four controls in turn (a fixed control panel).

### 2.6 Evaluation splits

Five splits define the training and test sets (Figure 1B). The future-years split (S1) tests all districts in later years. The held-out-states split (S2) leaves out whole states (departments in Colombia) in five folds. The held-out-regions split (S3) leaves out compact blocks of neighbouring districts and drops training districts within a buffer distance [@roberts2017cv; @ploton2020spatial; @wadoux2021spatialcv]. The buffer (225 km in Brazil, 130 km in Colombia) is the distance beyond which two districts' case counts, with the seasonal pattern removed, were no longer correlated above 0.1 in 2014–2019 (Supplementary Methods, section D3). The held-out-districts split (S4) assigns districts at random to five folds within states, and the held-out-country split (S5) trains on three countries and tests on the fourth. <!-- src: docs/methods/splits.md "Splits" (k-means blocks on centroids) and "Buffer distance D"; RC_repro_audit.md M6 -->

The three within-country spatial splits were run only in Brazil and Colombia, because Peru has too few provinces, giving 14 comparisons. <!-- src: docs/data/panel_weekly_v1.md (Peru 115 units); T1_primary_family.csv (14 cells: BR S1–S5, CO S1–S5, PE S1/S5, TW S1/S5) --> Held-out districts were never in a training fit, but they still supplied their own recent case counts at forecast time, so they were unseen but not information-free.

### 2.8 Scoring

The primary score was the weighted interval score (WIS) [@bracher2021wis] on log(1 + cases) [@bosse2023transformed]. It is a proper scoring rule [@gneiting2007proper]. It adds the width of each forecast interval to a penalty when the observation falls outside it. Lower is better, and for a single-value forecast it equals the absolute error. For an observation $y$ and a central interval from $l$ to $u$ with coverage $1-\alpha$, the interval score and the WIS are

$$\begin{aligned}
\mathrm{IS}_{\alpha}(l, u; y) &= (u - l) + \tfrac{2}{\alpha}\,(l - y)_{+} + \tfrac{2}{\alpha}\,(y - u)_{+},\\
\mathrm{WIS}(F, y) &= \frac{1}{K + 1/2}\Big(\tfrac{1}{2}\,\lvert y - m\rvert + \sum_{k=1}^{K} \tfrac{\alpha_k}{2}\,\mathrm{IS}_{\alpha_k}(l_k, u_k; y)\Big),
\end{aligned} \qquad (1)$$

where $F$ is the forecast, $y$ is the observed log(1 + cases), $m$ is the forecast median, and $(x)_{+}$ equals $x$ when $x$ is positive and 0 otherwise. Our nine quantiles give the median plus $K = 4$ central intervals, with bounds $l_k$ and $u_k$ and $\alpha_k$ = 0.8, 0.6, 0.4 and 0.2 for coverage 20%, 40%, 60% and 80%. Our WIS values are therefore not comparable with the 23-quantile WIS of forecast hubs.

Secondary measures were the WIS on the case scale and the coverage of the 40% and 80% intervals. We also split rows into three types of week: weeks with at least one case, zeros listed by the source (Brazil 2020–2021 only) and zero-filled weeks. Because these groups are defined by the observed outcome, they are descriptive only. A forecast that widens its upper tail tends to lose on zero weeks and gain on case weeks whether or not it is better overall (the forecaster's dilemma [@lerch2017dilemma]). We therefore also report measures computed from the forecasts alone.

### 2.9 Gains

Throughout, a positive gain means that the added input reduced forecast error, and a negative gain, which we call a loss, means that it increased error.

We write $\overline{W}_M$ for the mean WIS of model $M$ on the shared rows, with each target week weighted equally (Section 2.10). The embedding gain is how much the embeddings improve the reference model:

$$\Delta_{\text{emb}} = \overline{W}_{R} - \overline{W}_{R+E} \qquad (2)$$

where $R$ is the reference model (L3) and $R+E$ is the same model with the embeddings added (L4). The control gain is how much the selected location control alone improves the reference model:

$$\Delta_{\text{ctrl}} = \overline{W}_{R} - \overline{W}_{R+C} \qquad (3)$$

where $R+C$ is the reference model with the selected control (L3 + C\*). The excess gain is what the embeddings add once the model already has the selected control:

$$\Delta_{\text{excess}} = \overline{W}_{R+C} - \overline{W}_{R+C+E} \qquad (4)$$

where $R+C+E$ has both the control and the embeddings (L4 + C\*). It asks whether the embeddings add anything beyond where, or which, the district is.

The protocol named the excess gain as the primary measure. After review we treated the embedding gain and the excess gain as both main measures, because the selected control sometimes made the reference model worse (Section 3.3). In those comparisons, the excess gain measured the embeddings inside a weakened model. Gains are reported both as absolute differences and as percentages of $\overline{W}_R$, which differs widely between countries. The protocol also planned an identity share, $\Delta_{\text{ctrl}}/\Delta_{\text{emb}}$, to be reported only if the embedding gain's 95% confidence interval (CI) excluded zero and the gain was at least 1% of $\overline{W}_R$. These conditions were never met. <!-- src: docs/methods/eval_library.md estimands.py; RESOLUTION_LOG.md section B; T3_estimands.csv estimand ID_share, reason (not estimable in any comparison) -->

### 2.10 Statistical analysis

The estimate is the mean over target weeks of the WIS difference, averaged over districts and then over horizons 1 and 4. <!-- src: src/pop/eval/estimands.py infer_estimand --> We compared models with the Diebold–Mariano test [@diebold1995comparing], which asks whether the average weekly difference in score between two models is larger than chance would produce. Because errors in neighbouring weeks are correlated, we used a variance estimate built for correlated series (the equal-weighted cosine, EWC, estimator [@lazarus2018har]) and the Harvey–Leybourne–Newbold small-sample correction [@harvey1997testing]. <!-- src: src/pop/eval/stats.py ewc_inference; T1_primary_family.csv df (BR 16, CO 11, PE 13, TW 16) --> With Holm's correction for multiple testing [@holm1979simple], this is the main test. It treats weeks as the units of observation, so its conclusions apply to these districts and seasons, with each fitted model taken as fixed. The main test can reject too often when differences drift across seasons (Section 4.5), so we rely most on results that also hold under the two stricter checks below.

Each of the two main measures forms its own family of 14 comparisons for the Holm correction; only the excess-gain family was fixed before the held-out-place results were seen. <!-- src: T1_primary_family.csv n_family_available 14, family_complete True -->

To show that two models differ by very little, we used two one-sided tests (TOST) [@lakens2017equivalence]. A comparison was called equivalent when both one-sided null hypotheses were rejected after Holm adjustment over the 14 comparisons. Before adjustment, this means that the 90% CI of its gain lies inside ±1% of the reference model's WIS. Table 2 marks comparisons that were equivalent before adjustment only. The 1% value was the protocol's threshold for a material embedding gain; we adopted it as the margin after the Brazil and Peru future-years results had been seen. <!-- src: docs/plans/deviations_from_protocol.md row 9 ("Partly: Brazil S1 and Peru S1 results were already seen"); docs/plans/paper1_review_response.md (ID share only if G_emb ≥ 1% of WIS(L3)) --> We report ±0.5% alongside and ±2% as a sensitivity analysis [@isager2026tst]. A loss or gain requires a Holm-adjusted p below 0.05; a loss is small when its estimate lies inside the margin.

Two stricter checks repeat the tests (results in Table S26; their calibration in Table S12). The strict-variance check uses fewer variance terms (B = 4), which is safer when differences drift slowly between seasons. The surrogate-series check calibrates p values on simulated series that keep the pattern of our data (IAAFT surrogates). Further checks treat the reference WIS as an estimate, score on the case scale, or score all rows. A post hoc stress test rescored the forecasts after changing a share of zero-filled test weeks to one or three cases (Table S56). <!-- src: docs/results/review/R5_zero_fill_stress.md, Method (zero-filled targets only; shares 0.5%, 1%, 2%, 2.1%, 5% and 10%; 1 or 3 cases; 20 draws; stored forecasts rescored, no model refitted); deviations row 54 (commit 40d7faf) --> Unless stated, the checks and sensitivity analyses (Table S13) are not adjusted for multiplicity. <!-- src: docs/plans/deviations_from_protocol.md rows 28 and 33; RC_summary.md verdict; T6a_sensitivities.csv -->
